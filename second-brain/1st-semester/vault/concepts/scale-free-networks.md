## One-line Summary
A scale-free network has a degree distribution following a power law $P(k) \sim k^{-\gamma}$, meaning a few hub nodes have vastly more connections than most — and the network has no characteristic "typical" degree.

## Core Intuition
In [[random-graphs|Erdős-Rényi random graphs]], degree follows a Poisson distribution: most nodes have roughly the same degree, and very high-degree nodes are exponentially rare. Real networks look different: the Internet, the Web, airline networks, and citation networks all have a few highly connected hubs and many poorly connected nodes.

This is a **power-law** (or heavy-tailed) degree distribution: $P(k) \sim k^{-\gamma}$. The key consequence is that hubs act as shortcuts, making distances even shorter than the [[small-world-property|logarithmic prediction]] for random graphs. In networks like Facebook, celebrity hubs with millions of connections reduce the average path length below $\log n / \log k$.

## Formal Definition / Statement
**Scale-free network:** A network whose degree distribution follows a power law:
$$P(k) \sim k^{-\gamma}$$

where $\gamma$ is the degree exponent (typically $2 < \gamma < 3$ for real networks).

**Properties of power-law degree:**
- No characteristic scale: there is no "typical" degree
- Moments $\langle k^m \rangle$ diverge for $m \geq \gamma - 1$ when $\gamma \leq 3$
- Heavy tail: high-degree nodes (hubs) are much more likely than in Poisson distributions
- Self-similarity: the distribution looks the same at any scale

**Examples of scale-free networks:**
| Network | $\gamma$ (in-degree) |
|---|---|
| Web (hyperlinks) | ~2.1 |
| Internet (AS level) | ~2.2 |
| Citation networks | ~3.0 |
| Actor collaboration | ~2.3 |

## Key Properties / Complexity
- **Hubs**: a small number of nodes with very high degree act as shortcuts, reducing average path length
- **Robustness to random failure**: removing random nodes rarely hits hubs, so the network stays connected
- **Vulnerability to targeted attack**: removing hubs fragments the network rapidly
- **Distances shorter than $\log n / \log k$**: hubs provide "express lanes" across the network
- **Generated by [[preferential-attachment|preferential attachment]]**: new nodes prefer to connect to already well-connected nodes (rich-get-richer)

## Worked Example
Compare two networks with $n = 10^6$, average degree $k = 10$:

**Random graph (Poisson degree):**
$$\bar{d} \approx \frac{\log 10^6}{\log 10} = 6$$

**Scale-free ($\gamma = 2.5$):**
Hubs with degree $\sim 10^3$–$10^4$ exist. These create direct shortcuts between distant parts of the network. Empirical distances are often $\bar{d} \approx 3\text{–}4$, shorter than the random-graph prediction.

## Common Pitfalls
- **"Scale-free means all nodes have the same degree"** — The opposite: it means there IS no typical degree. The distribution spans many orders of magnitude.
- **"Power law = scale-free"** — Strictly, "scale-free" refers to the self-similarity property. Many networks are approximately power-law over a limited range but have cutoffs.
- **"All real networks are scale-free"** — Many are, but not all. Some have exponential or log-normal degree distributions. The claim is empirical, not universal.
- **"Watts-Strogatz produces scale-free networks"** — It does NOT. W-S preserves the narrow degree distribution of the initial lattice. See [[preferential-attachment]] for the model that generates scale-free networks.
- **"Power law implies $\gamma > 2$"** — For $\gamma \leq 2$, the mean degree diverges, which is physically unrealistic for finite networks.
