## One-line Summary
A semiconductor is a material with medium resistivity that can act as either a conductor or insulator, enabling controllable flow of electric charge.

## Core Intuition
Semiconductors sit between conductors and insulators — they're "bad conductors" whose conductivity can be precisely controlled. This controllability is what makes them the foundation of all modern electronics. By adding tiny amounts of impurities ([[doping]]), we can switch them between conducting and non-conducting states.

## Formal Definition / Statement
A semiconductor is a material with electrical resistivity between that of a [[conductor]] and an [[insulator]]. Its conductivity can be controlled through temperature, light, or introduction of impurity atoms ([[doping]]). The key property is a [[bandgap]] that is small enough to allow thermal excitation of electrons from the [[valence-band]] to the [[conduction-band]].

## Key Properties / Complexity
- Medium resistivity (between conductors and insulators)
- Small [[bandgap]] (typically 0.1–4 eV)
- Conductivity increases with temperature (opposite of metals)
- Can be modified by [[doping]] → [[n-type-semiconductor]] or [[p-type-semiconductor]]
- Enables binary states (conducting/non-conducting) for digital logic
- Primary material: [[silicon]] (Si)

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming semiconductors always conduct — pure (intrinsic) semiconductors are poor conductors
- Confusing semiconductors with conductors — semiconductor conductivity is controllable, conductor conductivity is fixed

## Connections
- [[silicon]] — the most important semiconductor material
- [[bandgap]] — determines whether a material is a conductor, semiconductor, or insulator
- [[intrinsic-semiconductor]] — pure semiconductor without doping
