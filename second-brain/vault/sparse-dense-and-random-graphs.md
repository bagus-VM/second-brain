## One-line Summary
Graphs differ quantitatively in edge density — from sparse (few edges) to dense (many edges) to random (probabilistic edge placement).
![[Pasted image 20260623143016.png]]
## Core Intuition
Not all graphs are created equal in terms of edge count. A social network with millions of users but average 150 friends is sparse. A complete graph on 100 nodes is dense. Random graphs generated by probabilistic rules serve as baseline models for comparison. This quantitative distinction affects which [[graph-representations|representations]] and algorithms are practical.

## Formal Definition / Statement
- ==**Sparse graph**: A graph with relatively few edges compared to the maximum possible number== |E| << |V|²
- **Dense graph**: ==A graph with many edges, often close to the maximum possible number== |E| ≈ |V|²
- **Random graph**: A graph generated by a probabilistic rule, e.g., each possible edge appears independently with probability p (==Erdős–Rényi model==)

## Key Properties
- Many real social and information networks are **sparse** — most people are not connected to most other people
- Dense graphs are often easier to reason about theoretically, but rarer at scale
- Random graphs are important as a **baseline model** for comparison with real networks
- Sparsity determines which [[graph-representations|representations]] are efficient: [[graph-representations|adjacency lists]] excel for sparse graphs, while [[graph-representations|adjacency matrices]] are natural for dense graphs
- A graph with n nodes has at most n(n-1)/2 edges (undirected) or n(n-1) edges (directed)

## Worked Example
Compare three 1000-node graphs:
1. **Sparse**: Average degree 10 → |E| ≈ 5,000 (0.1% of maximum)
2. **Dense**: Average degree 500 → |E| ≈ 250,000 (50% of maximum)
3. **Random (Erdős–Rényi)**: p = 0.01 → expected |E| ≈ 5,000

The sparse graph and random graph have similar edge counts, but the random graph's structure is governed by probability, not real-world constraints.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming all real networks are sparse — some (like brain connectivity) can be relatively dense
- Treating random graphs as realistic models — they lack clustering, community structure, and degree heterogeneity found in real networks
- Confusing "sparse" with "small" — a graph can be huge but still sparse
