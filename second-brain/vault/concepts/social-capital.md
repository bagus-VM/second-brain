## One-line Summary
Social capital is the advantage individuals gain from their network position — closure creates trust and enforcement, while bridging creates access to non-redundant information.

## Core Intuition
Network structure creates two kinds of value. Dense, closed neighborhoods (high [[clustering-coefficient]]) generate *bonding* social capital: trust, cooperation, mutual enforcement of norms. Bridging positions across [[structural-holes]] generate *bridging* social capital: access to diverse information, early exposure to opportunities, control over information flow. These two forms are in tension — more closure means less bridging and vice versa. The lecture's strong/weak tie framework maps directly onto this: strong ties create bonding capital, weak ties (as [[bridges-and-local-bridges|local bridges]]) create bridging capital.

## Formal Definition / Statement
**Social capital** (Putnam 2000): features of social organization — networks, norms, and trust — that facilitate coordination and cooperation for mutual benefit.

**Bonding social capital:**
- Generated by dense, closed networks (high [[clustering-coefficient]])
- Strong ties create trust, emotional support, norm enforcement
- Example: close-knit team with shared identity

**Bridging social capital:**
- Generated by sparse, bridging positions (low [[neighborhood-overlap]])
- Weak ties provide access to non-redundant information
- Example: person connected to multiple distinct communities
- Measured by Burt's constraint/effective size (see [[structural-holes]])

**Coleman (1988):** Closure in a network creates obligations, expectations, and enforcement — the foundation of trust. Without closure, there is no accountability.

**Granovetter (1973):** Weak ties provide access to information and opportunities outside one's immediate circle (see [[weak-ties-hypothesis]]).

## Key Properties
- Bonding and bridging capital are complementary, not substitutes
- Too much bonding → echo chambers, information redundancy
- Too much bridging → lack of trust, no enforcement of norms
- The optimal network structure depends on context (innovation vs. cooperation)
- Social capital is not equally distributed — some positions are inherently advantaged

## Worked Example
**Lecture scenario:**
- Within Team A, strong ties create bonding capital (trust, cooperation, shared knowledge)
- The Dia–Fin weak tie creates bridging capital (access to Team B's information)
- Dia has both: bonding capital within Team A, bridging capital to Team B
- This dual position makes Dia structurally advantageous

**Empirical (Bakshy et al. 2012):**
- Strong ties on Facebook are more *influential* per exposure (bonding capital → trust)
- Weak ties generate most *total* diffusion because they are numerous and carry novel content (bridging capital → access)

## Common Pitfalls
- Treating social capital as a single quantity — bonding and bridging are distinct
- Assuming more ties = more capital — redundant ties add bonding but not bridging capital
- Confusing social capital with network centrality — centrality measures position, capital measures *value* from position
- Thinking social capital is purely individual — it has community-level effects (Putnam)
